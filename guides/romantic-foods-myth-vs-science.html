'Romantic' Foods: What History Says and What Science Actually Supports

Oysters, chocolate, strawberries and chili have reputations as foods of love. Here's the history behind them and what the evidence really shows.

By the Gastrosex.com editors. Updated November 2026.

Almost every culture has foods associated with love and celebration. Some reputations come from scarcity and luxury, some from shape or color, and a few from chemistry. Here's an honest look at the most famous ones. The short version: no food works like a magic potion, but the right food in the right setting genuinely changes how an evening feels.

Chocolate

The story: The Aztec emperor Moctezuma was said to drink large quantities of cacao, and European courts later treated chocolate as an exotic luxury.

The science: Chocolate contains compounds such as phenylethylamine and theobromine, but in amounts too small, or too quickly broken down, to have a meaningful effect on mood. What chocolate reliably does is give pleasure: its melting point is just below body temperature, which is why it feels so luxurious in the mouth. That sensory pleasure is real and worth using, as in Molten Chocolate Lava Cakes for Two.

Oysters and shellfish

The story: Oysters have been a luxury food since Roman times and carry centuries of romantic legend.

The science: Oysters are extraordinarily rich in zinc, a mineral the body needs, but eating them doesn't produce an immediate effect on anyone well nourished. Their real power is occasion: shellfish feels special. Seared Scallops with Lemon Brown Butter gives you the same sense of luxury without shucking.

Strawberries

The story: Their red color and heart shape linked strawberries to Venus, the Roman goddess of love.

The science: None needed. They are sweet, fragrant, beautiful and easy to share, which is why Chocolate-Dipped Strawberries remain a classic.

Chili

The story: Spicy food has long been associated with passion and warmth.

The science: Capsaicin triggers a mild pain response, raising heart rate and prompting the release of endorphins. That's why spicy food can feel exhilarating. The effect is real but brief, and it is mostly about excitement and a shared experience. Go gently: not everyone enjoys heat.

Honey, figs and saffron

These appear in ancient texts and wedding traditions around the world, largely because they were precious, sweet or rare. Their value is symbolic and sensory, not pharmacological.

What actually makes a meal feel romantic

Researchers who study food and emotion keep returning to the same factors: anticipation, effort, novelty, shared experience and atmosphere. A meal someone cooked for you, eaten slowly by warm light, with someone who's paying attention to you, is the most reliable "romantic food" there is. Be wary of products or supplements sold as food-based mood or attraction boosters. Claims like these are rarely backed by good evidence. If you have health questions, talk to a qualified professional.
