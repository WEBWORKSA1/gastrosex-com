What Is a Gastrosexual? The Rise of Cooking to Impress

Where the word 'gastrosexual' came from, what the original research found, and why cooking has become one of the most attractive skills you can learn.

By the Gastrosex.com editors. Updated November 2026.

"Gastrosexual" sounds like a joke, but it started life as a serious piece of consumer research. In 2008 the London trend-forecasting firm the Future Foundation, working with the food brand PurAsia, published a report called The Emergence of the Gastrosexual. It combines gastronomy with the idea of attraction and describes people who cook not because they have to, but for pleasure, creativity and the admiration of the people they cook for.

What the original study found

The research profiled the typical gastrosexual as a man aged roughly 25 to 44, upwardly mobile and well travelled, who treats cooking as a hobby rather than a chore. Two numbers made the headlines at the time: around half of people surveyed said being able to cook makes someone more attractive, and nearly a quarter of men aged 18 to 34 said they cook partly to impress a partner. The report also tracked a long-term shift in who cooks at home, with men spending several times longer in the kitchen each day than they did in the early 1960s.

Coverage at the time, including in FSR Magazine and The Food Channel, linked the trend to celebrity chefs who made cooking look confident, creative and social.

Gastrosexual today: anyone who cooks to impress

Almost two decades later, the gender framing feels dated. Cooking to impress is something everyone does: the friend who throws the best dinner parties, the partner who plans a surprise anniversary menu, the new couple trading signature dishes. What has lasted is the core idea, and it is the reason this site exists. Cooking for someone is one of the most generous, memorable things you can do for them.

At Gastrosex.com we use the word in that broad, modern sense: a gastrosexual is anyone who uses food to say "I made an effort for you."

Why cooking is such an attractive skill

  • It shows care. A meal takes planning, shopping and time. Your guest feels that investment.
  • It shows competence under pressure. Getting three courses out hot and on time is quietly impressive.
  • It creates a shared experience. Restaurants are about being served; a home meal is about being together.
  • It is personal. You can cook around someone's favorite flavors, dietary needs and memories in a way no restaurant can.

How to become a confident cook-to-impress host

  1. Master three dishes, not thirty. One starter, one main and one dessert you can cook from memory beats an ambitious menu you are trying for the first time. Good candidates: Seared Scallops with Lemon Brown Butter, Pan-Seared Ribeye with Red Wine Pan Sauce and Molten Chocolate Lava Cakes for Two.
  2. Choose at least one make-ahead course. Desserts like Tiramisu Cups (No Raw Eggs) free up your attention for your guest.
  3. Plan backwards from the time you want to eat. Our Dinner Timeline Planner does the math for you.
  4. Think beyond the plate. Lighting, music and a clear table matter as much as the food. See Set the Scene: Table, Lighting and Music for a Dinner at Home.
  5. Ask about allergies and preferences in advance. It's thoughtful and it avoids disasters. Read Cooking for a Date With Allergies or Dietary Needs.

A note on the name

"Gastrosexual" is a descriptive term from published research, not a brand. Gastrosex.com is an independent food publication and is not affiliated with the original study's authors or sponsors. Everything on this site is family-friendly and about one thing: food that brings people together.
